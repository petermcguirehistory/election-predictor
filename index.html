Predicting the rest

Given what has been called and counted, where does every uncalled race stand now?

μrest | obs = μrest + Σro (Σoo + τ²I)−1 (obs − μo)

  • Observed — the counted margin in every race at least 20% in, and the sign alone in every called race without a count. A count's doubt grows with the share still out: about a quarter of a point when fully counted, ±3 at 93% in, ±6 at 87%, ±18 at 60%, ±31 at 30%.
  • Where it stops being the right tool — the last few percent of a count. A margin at 97% in is not a measurement with an error bar, it is a lead in ballots, and the prior this conditioning still blends it with can pull a lead of under a point across zero. Chamber totals come from here; a mostly-counted race is read from its own ballots instead, in What the ballots say.
  • Why early counts cannot stampede it — the gap between votes counted first and votes counted last is the same kind of gap everywhere in a state, and much of the country counts the same kind first. That shared early-count lean is a latent term in the conditioning, not independent noise, so three hundred half-counted races cannot together pin the national swing to their early-vote lean. As the counts complete the term vanishes and the count is the result.
  • Moved — every uncalled race, through the covariance the model itself put between races: the shared national term, the regional term and the state term. A Florida district running five points left of its forecast moves the rest of Florida most, the South next, and everything a little.
  • Why not just keep the matching simulations — that is what holding a call fixed does, and it is exact; but every close call halves the pool, and by mid-evening nothing survives. This conditions the joint as a Gaussian instead, and control is recounted from fresh draws of it.

What the ballots say

For every uncalled race with a count: how many ballots are left, what they would have to run, and whether that is plausible.

final lead = counted lead + ballots still out × their lean

  • Still out — AP's remainder, corrected by how far its expected totals have missed tonight at the same stage, measured from the races that have finished: the final count has landed below the expected total in most of them. Once AP pins a race at 99% the remainder is a placeholder, and the reading assumes 0.3–2% of the count is still to come in provisionals, cures and overseas ballots.
  • Needs — the margin the trailing side must win the remainder by. The late ballots' lean tonight is measured for its spread about the running margin, not its direction: the lean of the ballots counted so far barely predicts the lean of the rest.
  • Recount range — a lead under half a percent of a nearly complete count is a question of provisionals and recounts, and is labelled one rather than priced.
  • Now — from the ballots once 80% of the vote is in; from the conditioning above before that. The map, the headline and the tug of war use the same number.

Bluer or redder than 2024

Compared with how the same places voted for president two years ago: how many moved toward the Democrats, how many toward the Republicans, and by how much?

shift = counted margin − 2024 presidential margin, same district or state

  • Baseline — Harris minus Trump in 2024, on the 2026 district lines where a state redrew, and the statewide vote for Senate and governor races. It is the one number every race has. A midterm electorate is smaller than a presidential one, so a shift is partly who turned out and partly who changed their mind; Who turned out separates the two.
  • Counted — a race enters once 90% of its expected vote is in. Before that a margin says more about which precincts report first than about the electorate.
  • By votes cast — the median treats every race alike; the vote-weighted shift is the shift of the ballots themselves, which a big district moves more than a small one.
  • Forecast expected — the same subtraction with the model's median margin in place of the count, so the two can be read side by side.

Who turned out

How much of the 2024 electorate came back, where it held up, where it fell away, and whether the places that lost the most voters are the ones that moved?

turnout = 2026 votes ÷ 2024 presidential votes, same district or state

  • 2026 votes — the count once AP reports a race complete; AP's expected total while it is still being counted, which has run a little high tonight.
  • Harris and Trump districts — the same ratio over the districts each carried in 2024. If one side's voters stayed home, it shows here before it shows in any margin.
  • Against 2022 — the last midterm's House vote by state, where every district in the state is in and none went uncontested. The presidential comparison says who came back; the midterm comparison says whether this was a big midterm.
  • Turnout against shift — a district that kept its voters and still moved is a change of mind; one that lost half of them is a change of electorate.

The national polling

What happens to the forecast if the polls are off?

The generic ballot is the largest single input, and it leans:

  • Leverage — 13 pts of House control per point it is wrong.

Shared error

When the model is wrong about one race, is it wrong about the others?

Usually yes. Each race's error is four terms, three of them shared:

σtotal = √(σnat² + σreg² + σstate² + σidio²)

  • σnat — a national miss, shared by every race in the country.
  • σreg — shared across the region.
  • σstate — shared across the state. Worth 3.39 points.
  • σidio — this contest alone. Student-t, 5 degrees of freedom.

The model, end to end

What happens between a poll and a seat count?

  • Two estimates of each margin — a corrected poll average, and a prior built without polls. Every race has a prior; most have no polls.
  • Blended by how much polling there is, then simulated with errors shared nationally, by region and by state, and counted.

Each box links to the section that shows what was fitted for it.

    Checks against past elections

    Has this model been right before?

    • Refitted without the scored election — pollster ratings, house effects and the polling error curve.
    • Fitted with it — the prior's width, the state error term, elasticity and the generic-ballot correction, each pooled over the same cycles the House replays score. Those replays are partly in sample, and read slightly kinder than a clean hold-out would.

    What could be wrong

    What does this model say is wrong with it?

    • Alarms — recomputed every run; they come and go with the data.
    • Limitations — structural; they clear only when the model changes.

    What the model is working from

    How much of this is polling, and how much is inference?

    Mostly inference: almost nobody polls a safe district. An unpolled race runs on its prior, with a wider error bar.
