Election night is simulated 20,000 times. All 506 races are drawn together in each run, so a polling miss in one place carries into the others.

  • The headline is runs won ÷ 20,000 — a probability, not a prediction.
  • 70% means 70 runs in 100 won, 30 lost. Both halves are the forecast.
  • Calibration — whether stated probabilities have matched observed hit rates — is on the Can you trust it tab.

Every seat, in order

Where does the majority fall, and which seats sit on it?

Every seat in the chamber, lined up as one strip:

  • Order — safest Democratic seat at the left, safest Republican at the right, by the race's median margin. Seats not on this year's ballot sit at the ends.
  • Colour — P(D wins). Bright blue or red is a safe seat, grey is a toss-up. Hatched seats are not on the ballot.
  • The outlined seat — the seat whose rank equals the majority, 218 in the House and 51 in the Senate. If each side wins every seat on its own end of the strip, this race decides the chamber.
  • Not the same as the deciding race — this is one fixed ordering. In each simulation the order is different, so the race that most often casts the deciding vote is a separate count, in What to watch.

Below the strip, the seats nearest that line are drawn large enough to read. Click any seat for that race's full working.

Seats that could change hands

Which seats are most likely to flip, in any chamber?

A seat flips when the party holding it going into November loses it. House, Senate and governor races share one list here, ranked by that chance alone:

P(flip) = P(holder loses) = 1 − P(D) for a Democratic seat, P(D) for a Republican one

  • Held by — the party holding the seat today. Where the only challenger is an independent, a flip is a win by that independent.
  • Expected flips — Σ P(flip) over the chamber. An average, not a night: in any single simulation each seat flips entirely or not at all, so the range beside it is counted from the simulations themselves.
  • Net — Σ P(flip | R-held) − Σ P(flip | D-held): the expected change in the non-Republican side's seats. An independent's win counts against whichever party held the seat.
  • Redrawn — the district's lines changed for 2026. The holder is whoever holds that district number now, so part of a flip there is the new map.

What to watch

Which races is this resting on?

A race belongs here in proportion to how much the answer rests on it, which is not the same as how close it is. Two ranking quantities, depending on whether a majority is at stake:

  • Tipping-point share — how often the race casts the vote that makes the majority, counted across all 20,000 runs. Not guessed from the margin.
  • Closeness — distance from an even chance. The 36 governorships confer no majority, so no governor's race can be the deciding seat; they are ranked this way instead.

Each list names the quantity it used. Beneath it, the same share for the top races as bars: the deciding vote is spread across many races, because the ordering of seats is different in every simulation. Click any row or bar for that race's full working: the ballot, the prior, the polls, and the weight given to each.

Where are the seats, and how much does the model know about each one?

Colour carries whichever quantity is selected above — by default, each side's chance of winning the seat: blue for Democrats, red for Republicans, deeper where the seat is safer. Three controls:

  • Cartogram / Boundaries — equal-area hexes, or real district shapes.

What moved it

Why is this number different from last time?

A move is either news about the electorate or a change in the model, and its size does not say which. So each cause is priced by re-running the forecast with only that input changed and every other input held fixed. The causes sum to the movement shown under each chamber's card on the Who wins tab.

The national polling

What happens to the forecast if the polls are off?

The generic ballot — which party's candidate would you vote for in your district? — is the model's largest single input, and it has a measured lean.

  • Direction — it has overstated Democrats in 10 of the last 13 cycles.
  • Size — 2.74 points on average, subtracted before use.
  • Leverage — 13 points of House control per point the correction is wrong, read off the sweep below. It moves run to run, because the sweep is steepest where the chamber is closest.

The correction is an estimate from 13 past elections, not a measurement of this one. The slider re-runs the entire forecast at a different assumed lean.

Shared error

When the model is wrong about one race, is it wrong about the others?

Usually yes, and that shared error dominates the uncertainty. If the polls are three points too Democratic in one Pennsylvania district, they are probably about three points too Democratic in Ohio and Michigan. Treating 435 races as 435 independent coin flips would price a 40-seat swing as near-impossible; the country produces one about every decade.

So each race carries four independent error terms, added in quadrature — the largest dominates:

σtotal = √(σnat² + σreg² + σstate² + σidio²)

  • σnat — a national miss, shared by every race in the country.
  • σreg — shared across the region.
  • σstate — shared across the state. Worth 3.39 points.
  • σidio — this contest alone. Student-t, 5 degrees of freedom.

The House map

How far is the map from the country it is drawn on?

Lean is a district's 2024 presidential margin measured against the national margin the same day: lean = district margin − national margin. R+5 is five points right of wherever the country lands, not five points Republican outright. Order all 435 and the 218th decides the House.

The 218th sits 3.26 points right of the country — a price of entry. Democrats must carry the national vote by about that much before the median district is theirs; short of it they lead the country and lose the chamber.

The state borders

And how far are the Senate and the governorships?

Both are decided by states, and a state's weight is independent of its population: Wyoming and California send two senators each. Scoring the same 2024 presidential result by state instead of by district says how far that arrangement sits from the country.

A separate section, because it is a different kind of fact. District lines were drawn and can be redrawn. Equal representation in the Senate is the one provision Article V puts beyond amendment without a state's own consent.

Out-of-sample checks

Has this model been right before?

Every check here is out of sample: to score the model against a past election it is first rebuilt with that election removed from everything it was fitted on. It never grades a test it has already seen.

Which checks appear follows the scope control above, because the evidence differs by chamber.

  • House — three past elections (2018, 2020, 2022) replayable district by district.
  • Senate and governors — no replayable elections, so their cards report what was measured about them directly. One of those measurements was taken and deliberately not acted on.

Every card names its own source.

What could be wrong

What does this model say is wrong with it?

Two kinds, kept apart because they behave differently.

  • Alarms — recomputed every run, and they come and go with the data: a feed going quiet, a race average resting on one poll.
  • Limitations — structural. Established when the model was built; they clear only when somebody finds the missing data or changes the model.

Both are on the page, not behind a methodology link.

What the model is working from

How much of this is polling, and how much is inference?

Mostly inference, which is normal for a midterm: almost no one polls a safe district. An unpolled race runs on its prior — the seat's presidential lean, the national environment and whether an incumbent is running — with a wider error bar to match. These are the counts.

Payload diagnostics

Is the page showing what the engine actually computed?

The full simulation is too large to send to a browser, so the payload is one bit per race per draw: who won, 20,000 times over. Every total on this page is counted back out of those bits in your browser. These checks confirm the counting still reproduces the engine's own answers exactly. A drift would leave the numbers looking entirely plausible, so it is checked rather than assumed.
