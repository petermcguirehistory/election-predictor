A probability, not a prediction. The model plays election night out 20,000 times, drawing all 506 races together in each run so that a polling miss in one place carries into the others the way a real one would, and the headline is the share of those runs in which a party ends up in control. A 70% is not a forecast that the party wins — it is a forecast that they win in about 70 runs out of every 100 and lose in the other 30, and those 30 are as much a part of the answer as the 70. Whether that has held up in practice is the Track record tab.

What moved it

Why is this number different from last time?

A forecast that changes is either news about the electorate or news about the model, and a reader cannot tell which from the size of the move. Not simply the closest ones: a race belongs here in proportion to how much the answer rests on it. The House, by district

Where are the seats, and how much does the model know about each one?

Colour carries whichever quantity is selected above — by default each side's chance of winning the seat, blue for Democrats and red for Republicans, deeper where the seat is safer. Colour is also drained toward grey wherever the model knows least: a seat with no usable polling is pulled in toward the middle of the scale, so a guess cannot be shown as boldly as a well-polled near-certainty. Where the seats come from

Which seats change hands?

Each race contributes its probability rather than a whole seat, so a district Democrats take in 60% of runs counts as 0.6 of a gain and not as one. Adding those up gives an expected net change — a summary of every simulation at once, not a picture of any single election night, on which seats flip in whole numbers.

Path to a majority

Which seat decides it?

Two ways of asking that, side by side, and they do not give the same answer. Ordered by margin lines every seat on the ballot up from safest Democratic to safest Republican and marks the one that reaches a majority — a single ordering, using each race's expected margin. Which race decides it asks instead how often each race actually casts the deciding vote across all the simulations, and the answer is spread over many races, because the order of the seats is different in every run.

One dot per race, placed at the margin the model expects — Democrats to the left of zero, Republicans to the right, safest seats at the outer edges. The House map

How far is the map from the country it is drawn on?

Every district is scored by how it voted for president in 2024, measured against how the whole country voted the same day. A district at R+5 is five points more Republican than the nation — not five points Republican outright, but five points to the right of wherever the country lands. Put all 435 in order from most Democratic to most Republican and the 218th is the one that decides the House.

That 218th district sits 3.26 points to the right of the country. Read it as a price of entry: Democrats have to carry the country by about three points before the median district is theirs, and anything short of that means leading the national vote and losing the chamber anyway.

The state borders

And how far are the Senate and the governorships?

Those are decided by states, and a state's weight has nothing to do with how many people live in it: Wyoming and California send two senators each. Scoring the same 2024 presidential result by state instead of by district says how far that arrangement sits from the country.

It is a different kind of fact from the one above, which is why it is a separate section rather than the same chart on a different unit. District lines were drawn by somebody and can be drawn again. State borders were not drawn for this purpose and are not going to move — equal representation in the Senate is the one provision Article V puts beyond amendment without a state's own consent.

The national polling

What happens to the forecast if the polls are off?

The generic ballot — which party's candidate would you vote for in your district? — is the model's most important single input, and it has a known lean. In 10 of the last 13 cycles it has overstated Democrats, by 2.74 points on average, so the model subtracts that before using it. That subtraction is an estimate from past elections, not a measurement of this one, and on this run it is worth about 13 points of House control for every point it turns out to be wrong — read off the sweep below, which is why the figure moves from run to run.

The slider re-runs the entire forecast at a different assumed lean, so you can see how much of the headline number rests on it.

Shared error

When the model is wrong about one race, is it wrong about the others?

Usually yes, and that is most of what makes forecasting hard. If the polls are three points too Democratic in one Pennsylvania district, they are probably about three points too Democratic in Ohio and Michigan too. A model that treated 435 races as 435 independent coin flips would report that a 40-seat swing is nearly impossible, when the country produces one every decade or so.

So each race carries four sources of error rather than one: a national miss shared by every race, a regional miss, a miss shared across the state — worth 3.39 points — and the race's own. The grid below is what that adds up to, measured off the simulations themselves rather than assumed.

Out-of-sample checks

Has this model been right before?

Every check on this page is out of sample: whenever the model is scored against a past election, it is rebuilt with that election removed from everything it was fitted on first. It never grades a test it has already seen. That is the difference between a track record and a model describing the data it was built from.

Which checks appear follows the scope control above, because the evidence is not the same for all three chambers. The House has three past elections that can be replayed district by district. The Senate and the governorships do not, so their cards show what was measured about them instead of borrowing the House's record — including one measurement that was taken and then deliberately not acted on.

Forecast history

How has this forecast moved over time?

Each point is a run that was published. The line is drawn solid only between two runs that used the same model on the same sources; where something in the build changed in between — a new prior source, a different poll feed, a different number of draws — the line is broken instead. A jump caused by the model changing is not news about the election, and joining it up would present it as though it were.

What could be wrong

What does this model say is wrong with it?

Two kinds of thing, kept apart because they behave differently. Alarms are recomputed on every run and come and go with the data — a feed going quiet, a race average resting on a single poll. Limitations are structural: they were established when the model was built and will not clear by themselves, only when somebody finds the missing data or changes the model. Both sit on the page rather than behind a methodology link, because a reader who never opens the methodology is exactly the one who needs to know how old the generic ballot is.

What the model is working from

How much of this is polling, and how much is inference?

Mostly inference, and that is normal for a midterm: almost no one polls a safe district. Unpolled races are not left out — they are forecast from how the seat voted for president, the national environment and whether an incumbent is running, with wider error bars to match. These are the counts.

Payload diagnostics

Is the page showing what the engine actually computed?

The full simulation is far too large to send to a browser, so what ships is one bit per race per draw: who won, 20,000 times over. Every total on this page is counted back out of those bits in your browser. These checks confirm that the counting still reproduces the engine's own answers exactly — if it ever stopped, the numbers would still look entirely plausible, which is why this is checked rather than assumed.
